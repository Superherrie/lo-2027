The Baroque in Italy: Caravaggio, Gentileschi, Bernini

Art history and South African art

  • Describe the work of Caravaggio, Gentileschi and Bernini
  • Analyse how Caravaggio uses light in one painting
Think first

If you wanted a Bible story to feel as if it were happening in your own street today, how would you paint it?

Words to know

Baroque
The dramatic, emotional style of the 1600s and early 1700s.
Tenebrism
Extreme chiaroscuro: figures emerge from deep darkness, lit by one strong light.
Counter-Reformation
The Catholic Church's response to the Reformation, including new guidance on art.
Dynamic
Full of energy and movement, often through diagonals and twisting forms.
Colonnade
A row of columns, like the curving arms around St Peter's Square.

Check these words in your Art Theory or Art History booklet.

Notes

Drama, movement and tenebrism; the Counter-Reformation; Bernini's sculpture and St Peter's.

Why the Baroque happened

  • The Council of Trent (1545 – 1563): The Catholic Church decided religious art should be clear, emotional and convincing, to inspire faith.
  • Drama and movement: Diagonals, spirals and twisting figures capture a decisive moment in a story.
  • The viewer is drawn in: Light and composition pull the viewer's space into the scene.
  • Ordinary people: Real-looking models and everyday details are used, even in sacred subjects.

Three Italian masters

  • Caravaggio (1571 – 1610): Biblical scenes with ordinary models in contemporary clothes, lit by a beam of light against darkness: tenebrism.
  • Artemisia Gentileschi (1593 – c. 1656): One of the first women in the Florentine Academy of Design (1616). Painted strong heroines such as Judith and Esther.
  • Bernini's David (1623 – 1624): Twists in the very moment of throwing, so the viewer feels part of the action.
  • The Ecstasy of Saint Teresa (1647 – 1652): Sculpture, architecture and hidden light combine like theatre to show the saint's vision of God's love.
  • St Peter's: Bernini's colonnade (1656 – 1667) reaches out like arms embracing the faithful; he also made the bronze canopy over the altar.
Remember

In the Baroque, light does not just show the story; it tells it.

Think about it (Matthew 9:9)

Jesus calls Matthew, a tax collector whom many people despised, and Matthew gets up and follows him. What does Caravaggio's ordinary room suggest about who God calls?

Practical

On your own · about 20 minutes

You need: History booklet, visual journal, image on screen

Caravaggio's light: The Calling of Saint Matthew

  1. Heading: Caravaggio, The Calling of Saint Matthew, 1599 – 1600, oil on canvas, Rome.
  2. Describe: who is in the room, what are they doing, and where is Christ?
  3. Find the light: draw a small thumbnail and mark the direction of the beam with an arrow.
  4. Analyse: write two identify, describe, effect sentences about tone (tenebrism) and direction.
  5. Interpret: what could the beam of light stand for in this story?
  6. Opinion: does the everyday setting make the story more or less powerful? Explain.

Test yourself

  1. Define tenebrism and name an artist known for it.
  2. Explain one way the Council of Trent influenced Baroque art.
  3. Compare Bernini's David with Michelangelo's David.
Show answers

1. Extreme chiaroscuro, with figures emerging from deep darkness; Caravaggio (also Gentileschi). 2. It asked for religious art that was clear, emotional and convincing, so artists used drama, real-looking people and strong light. 3. Michelangelo's David is calm and tense before the fight; Bernini's twists in the act of throwing and draws the viewer into the action.
