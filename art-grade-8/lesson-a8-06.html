Texture 1: drawing textures in graphite

Theory: elements, principles, colour, analysis

Start here

Today I will

  • Today I will tell the difference between actual and implied texture
  • Today I will use hatching, stippling, scumbling and short flicks to show a surface
  • Today I will draw nine labelled texture boxes in graphite
Think first

Close your eyes and touch your sleeve, then the desk. How could you show that difference with only a pencil?

Words to Know

WordMeaning
TextureAn element of art: how a surface feels, or looks as if it would feel, such as rough, smooth or bumpy.
Actual textureTexture you can really feel when you touch the surface of an artwork.
Implied textureTexture that looks real but is only drawn; the paper is actually flat.
HatchingShading with lines drawn close together in the same direction.
Cross-hatchingSets of hatching lines that cross each other; more layers make darker tones.
StipplingShading with many small dots; dots closer together make darker tones.

Find these words in your Art Vocabulary booklet.

Notes

Actual and implied texture; mark-making that shows texture (hatching, stippling, scumbling, short flicks).

Marks that make texture

  • Hatching: Parallel lines close together; follow the direction of the surface.
  • Cross-hatching: Hatching in two or more directions on top of each other for darker areas.
  • Stippling: Dots: many close dots for dark, few for light. Good for pebbles and stone.
  • Scumbling: Tiny overlapping scribbles or circles (circulism) for rough or soft surfaces.
  • Short flicks: Quick strokes that start dark and lift off: perfect for fur and feathers.

Which mark for which surface?

SurfaceMarks to try
Fish scales and brickRepeated curved or rectangular shapes, each with a darker edge on its shadow side.
Wood grain and tree barkLong wavy lines with knots (wood); broken vertical lines and deep cracks (bark).
Fur and feathersShort flicks in the direction of growth (fur); a centre line with fine curved strokes (feathers).
Rope, pebbles, crumpled paperTwisted diagonal bands (rope); stippled round forms (pebbles); flat planes with sharp creases (paper).
Remember

Repeat the right mark in the right direction and the eye feels the surface.

Practical

On your own · about 30 minutes

You need: Visual journal, HB, 2B, 4B and 6B pencils, eraser

Nine texture boxes

  1. Rule a 3 × 3 grid of nine boxes of about 5 cm.
  2. Label the boxes lightly underneath: fish scales, wood grain, brick, fur, feathers, tree bark, rope, pebbles, crumpled paper.
  3. Fill each box with the texture, choosing the mark that suits it best.
  4. Use at least four different marks across the nine boxes.
  5. Give each box a light area and a dark area so it does not look flat.
  6. Under each label, add the mark you used (for example 'fur: short flicks').
  7. This page is marked out of 10 (Texture boxes): nine different, convincing textures (5); the marks suit each surface (3); each box labelled with the texture and the mark (2).
Assessment

Marked practical (10 marks), Texture boxes: nine different, convincing textures (5); the marks suit each surface (3); each box labelled with the texture and the mark (2).

Test yourself

  1. What is the difference between actual and implied texture?
  2. How do you make stippling look darker?
  3. Which mark did you use for fur, and why?
Show answers

1. Actual texture can be felt; implied texture is drawn to look real but the paper is flat. 2. Put the dots closer together. 3. Usually short flicks in the direction the hair grows, because fur is made of many fine hairs.

Finished early?

Zoom-in texture (about 10 minutes)

Look closer and draw just one small part of your object, very large.

  1. Choose one scale, ridge or fold of your textured object.
  2. Draw it as large as your hand in your visual journal.
  3. Give it a light side, a mid-tone and a shadow side.
  4. Build the darks in layers and keep one highlight white.
